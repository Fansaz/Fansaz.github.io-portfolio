What it does

A two-person agency needed outbound that ran without anyone watching it. The system finds dental practices, writes personalised sequences, sends from six warmed mailboxes, reads what comes back, and routes genuine interest into the CRM while suppressing everyone who asks to be left alone.

  1. DiscoverApollo search filtered to independent practices, excluding dental groups over 50 staff, labs, schools and suppliers.
  2. QualifyEmail reveal, then a corroboration check that compares each contact's email domain against the company on file.
  3. AssignLeads split evenly across two copy variants so the offer can be measured against itself.
  4. SendNine emails over 64 days through Instantly, paced by a per-mailbox gap and a weekday sending window.
  5. ClassifyEvery reply labelled: opt-out, wrong person, bounce, out of office, objection, or genuine interest.
  6. RouteInterest creates a GoHighLevel contact, opportunity and booking link. Everything else suppresses or escalates to a human on Slack.

The state machine is the spine. Instantly knows what it sent; Postgres knows what that meant and what should happen next. A reconciler reads Instantly's own lead records every ten minutes and repairs any divergence, so the two never drift apart silently.

The dashboard

Built after an audit of the running system, on the brief that it be elegant, black and blue, easy on the eyes, and reachable any time the VPS is up. It is strictly read-only: it observes the pipeline and never commands it.

Problems found and fixed

Most of the engineering was not building features. It was finding the places where this system failed quietly, which is the only way an unattended one can fail.

Fixed

842 leads frozen since upload

The campaign lookup matched only keys ending in varA or varB. Live keys ended in a date, so the lookup returned nothing, the poller skipped those campaigns, and their leads never left QUEUED while Instantly emailed them for weeks.

The proof was one query: the entire event table held 46 send rows, matching exactly the 46 leads under the two legacy keys that did resolve. Fixed the match, backfilled 536 records, then added a reconciler so state repairs itself rather than depending on one lookup being right.

Fixed

A refusal read as a buying signal

The reply classifier matched \binterested\b, which also matches inside "not interested". A prospect declining would have been written into the CRM as a hot lead and sent a booking link.

Rewritten with explicit precedence, refusal tested before interest, and 43 tests pinning both directions.

Fixed

Every reply silently discarded

Inbound replies were matched to a lead by the message's to address. On a reply that is our own mailbox, not the prospect. No contact matched, and the function returned without a trace.

It stayed invisible for weeks because the reply table was empty, so there was nothing to notice missing. Found by checking a live opt-out rather than trusting the green cron status.

Fixed

An opt-out that spelled it "e-mail"

A dentist wrote "Please put me on your do not e-mail list." Every pattern said email; the hyphen meant no match, and it classified as a neutral reply. Caught by printing the classification before acting on it.

The first repair then over-corrected and matched "put me on the list for your webinar", which would have suppressed an interested prospect. Both directions are now tested.

Fixed

Suppression that suppressed nothing

The suppression table had one INSERT and no SELECT anywhere in the codebase. Opt-outs were recorded and then ignored on the next upload. Separately, the call meant to stop a booked prospect's sequence pointed at an endpoint that returns 404, so anyone who booked a meeting kept receiving cold follow-ups.

The upload path now reads the list, and stopping is done with a blocklist entry, the one mechanism that was verified to work.

Fixed

One in eight emails named the wrong business

Apollo had attached contacts to companies they did not work for. A lawyer at Quinn Law was linked to a practice called "Quinn Dental" and replied, reasonably, "I'm not a dentist."

Added a corroboration test comparing each lead's email domain against the company name by token, acronym and prefix. Where it fails, the merge field falls back to a generic phrase instead of confidently naming the wrong business. Six sentences had to be reworded so they read correctly either way.

Fixed

Emails arriving as one solid paragraph

Campaign bodies are rendered as HTML, where a newline is whitespace. Copy written with blank lines between paragraphs collapsed into a single block, and had been sending that way for weeks before anyone looked at a rendered preview.

The database keeps readable plain text; the paragraph markup is generated on the way out, so editing the copy later does not mean writing tags by hand.

Working safely on a live system

The system was sending to real practices throughout. Three habits did most of the work, and nearly every defect above was an instance of one of them being skipped.

Snapshot outside the blast radius

Before any destructive step, export the state to a file the operation cannot touch. Deleting the old campaigns destroyed their engagement history inside Instantly; the CSV written beforehand is why it still exists.

One, then ten, then the rest

Every bulk write ran as a canary, a small batch, then the remainder, with a checkpoint keyed on email. A rate-limit rejection partway through a 862-lead upload resumed exactly where it stopped.

Read back, don't trust the write

A create call returning 200 is not proof. Campaigns created as "paused" come back as drafts, so the status is read again before anything is loaded into them.

Dry run that prints the decision

Every destructive script runs read-only by default and prints what it would do. That is what caught the misclassified opt-out before it was acted on.
